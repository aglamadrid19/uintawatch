**Decade totals:** Over **1.2 million acres** burned across Utah in the past 10 years. In the last 3 years alone, **27% of Utah's total forest and woodland** area burned since 1986 has been consumed. An alarming **10% of Utah's high-elevation forest has burned within the past 10 years**.

The largest wildfire in Utah's recent recorded history was the **Milford Flat fire in 2007** at **363,052 acres** in Millard and Beaver Counties. The 2026 fire season is on pace to approach or exceed this record.

---

### Structural & Home Damage

- **2026 (season ongoing, through Aug):** **180+ structures destroyed**, nearly **250 destroyed or damaged** across large fires. The Cottonwood Fire alone destroyed **185+ structures** (including the Eagle Point ski resort). Federal incident reports list 200+ structures across all 2026 fires.
- **2025:** Dozens of primary and secondary homes lost from the Deer Creek, Forsyth, and Monroe Canyon fires. Smaller grass fires destroyed homes including a pair of apartment buildings in Millcreek. "We lost a lot more structures than in the last five years… It was an insane year for structure loss" — Kayli Guild, Utah Division of Forestry, Fire and State Lands.
- **2018 (Dollar Ridge Fire):** 80+ homes affected; 15 structures damaged; subsequent debris flows and mudslides in burn scar.
- **2017 (Brian Head Fire):** 13+ homes destroyed; cost exceeded **$15–$20 million**.
- **2026 (through Aug):** **3,773 structures threatened** by large fires across the state.
- **Historical:** In 2007, the Neola North fire resulted in **3 fatalities** alongside the 363,052-acre Milford Flat fire.

---

### Human Casualties

- **Wildfire-related civilian deaths in Utah (2000–2026):** At minimum **6 confirmed deaths** from the Neola North Fire (2007), with other fire season deaths under investigation.
- **On-duty firefighter deaths (Utah, 1990–2026):** **19 total**, including:
  - **3 firefighters killed June 27, 2026** (Snyder Fire, Utah-Colorado border): Emily Barker (USFS Rifle Helitack), Nick Hutcherson (Pittman Valley Helitack), Sydney Watson (US Wildland Fire Service).
  - 2 additional fatalities suspected from a helicopter crash near the Widemouth 2 Fire site (Aug 7, 2026).
  - Prior: Battalion Chief Mathew Burchett (2018, Ranch Fire, CA), Caleb Hamm (2011, CR 377 Fire, TX), Division Supervisor Spencer Koyle (2006, Devil's Den Fire, UT), SEAT pilot Wayne Turner (2004, Utah), and **2 inmate firefighters killed by lightning** (2000, North Stansbury Fire, UT).
- **Smoke-related health impacts:** A 2020 BYU study found air pollution costs Utah's economy **$1.8 billion** annually through healthcare expenses, lost earning potential, and tourism decline. Wildfire smoke is linked to a **34% increase in emergency department visits for respiratory diagnoses** and a **112% increase in ED visits for asthma**. Hospital visits for COPD, bronchitis, and emphysema rise significantly during wildfire events.

**Outdoor recreation industry:** Utah's outdoor recreation contributes **3.4% of Utah's GDP** and supports **nearly 72,000 jobs** (U.S. Dept. of Commerce Bureau of Economic Analysis). Tourism generates approximately **$13.3 billion annually**. The loss of recreation sites, trail access, and scenic areas due to wildfire directly threatens this economic driver. The state has seen post-fire flooding from the Cottonwood Fire (Beaver Canyon) and ongoing flooding from the 2025 Monroe Fire — both causing road washouts, watershed damage, and requiring **years of recovery**.

---

### Agricultural & Ranching Impact

- The **Widemouth 2 Fire alone killed approximately 100 cattle**. Farmers and ranchers face the loss of animals, grazing land, and operational resources — with recovery often taking years. Replacing livestock and reviving grazing land requires significant financial investment, and it can be several years before livestock and land are productive again.

> *"Wildfires can change the course of a ranching operation in a matter of hours, but recovery often takes years. Our livestock producers are facing the loss of animals, grazing land, and the resources they depend on to sustain their operations."*
> — Kelly Pehrson, Utah Department of Agriculture and Food Commissioner

- The Trail Mountain Fire (2018) **compromised springs that feed culinary water systems in Emery County**.
- Recent fires have threatened ranching operations statewide, including the Cottonwood, Babylon, Iron, Cherry, and Wild Goose fires.

---

### Long-Term Environmental Consequences

- **Flooding:** Post-fire monsoon rains create catastrophic flooding on soil stripped of vegetation. Experts warn flooding in burn scars can last **several years**. The Cottonwood Fire burn scar is already causing flooding in Beaver Canyon. Even areas not directly burned are impacted by downstream flooding.
- **Water quality:** Wildfires pollute water sources from ash and charred material for months to years after a fire. Panguitch residents were forced to **boil water for several days** after Brian Head Fire runoff polluted their drinking water.
- **Air quality:** The 2007 fire season produced **40 days of unhealthy air** (24 days more than the previous year), with dust storms creating hazardous driving conditions on I-15 for months in Millard County.
- **10% of Utah's high-elevation forest has burned within the last 10 years** — an unprecedented rate that's raising concerns about permanent ecosystem damage and flash flooding risks.
- Wildfire releases greenhouse gases into the atmosphere, contributing to climate change — a feedback loop that intensifies future fire risk.

## KEY TRENDS (2015–2026)

1. **Accelerating scale:** Three fires in 2026 each burned more than 60,000 acres of trees — a first in Utah history. Previously, only 7 fires since 1986 had exceeded that mark in total acreage.
2. **Forest and woodland destruction:** 27% of Utah's forest and woodland has burned in just the last 3 years (2024–2026).
3. **Human-caused fires dominate:** In 2026, human causes were suspected in **75% of wildfires**. Human-caused fires rose from ~49% to over 70% in recent years. Early detection and rapid reporting could significantly reduce this risk.
4. **Growing structure losses:** 2025 and 2026 represent two of the most destructive fire seasons for structures in Utah's recent history.
5. **Multi-year economic burden:** Firefighting costs, post-fire recovery, healthcare costs from smoke inhalation, and lost recreation/tourism revenue create a multi-year economic drag on the state.
6. **Expanding fire season:** The 2025–2026 winter was the warmest on record with one of the lowest recorded snowpacks, leaving fuels dry enough to foster fire conditions **as early as March**. The traditional July–October fire season is expanding at both ends.
