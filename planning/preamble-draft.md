# Uinta Watch

## The watch begins in the wildlands

Wildfires have consumed more than **1.2 million acres** across Utah over the past decade, and the seasons keep getting worse. In 2026 alone, fires have destroyed over **180 structures**, killed **3 on-duty firefighters**, and burned a record area of Utah's forests and woodlands. The 2025 fire season cost Utah **$240 million**. The 2026 season — still not over — is tracking to exceed it. Long after the flames die, the ash, the flooding, and the empty spaces where homes used to stand remain. Utahns are still living with the consequences from fires that burned last year, and the year before that.

What turns a small fire into a catastrophic one is usually **time** — how long it burns before anyone knows it exists.

In 2018, the Dollar Ridge Fire swept through a rural community in a matter of hours. In 2017, the Brian Head Fire burned for over a month, destroying 13 homes and threatening entire towns. In 2026, the Cottonwood Fire consumed nearly **97,000 acres** and more than 150 structures in a single week. In every case, the difference between a small fire and a catastrophic one came down to how quickly the fire was detected and reported before it spread.

Today, the first person to spot smoke on a remote ridge is often a hiker, a rancher, or a neighbor. There is no systematic way for those people — or for any automated sensor — to get that information into the emergency network fast enough to matter. The gap between smoke and response is still measured in hours.

Utah's rural wildlands are vast and beautiful, but they're also under-monitored. Cell towers don't reach every ridge, satellites capture images that can be hours old by the time they're processed, and professional fire crews are stretched across the entire western United States. The people best placed to notice a new plume are the ones who happened to be there — and right now, there's no systematic way for them to become part of the warning system.

**Uinta Watch exists to change that.**

## Built by the community, for the community

Uinta Watch is a community-driven early wildfire detection network. The idea is simple: **put the tools for early detection in the hands of the people who are already out there**.

We're building an open-source, solar-powered mesh network of fire-detection sensors that can be deployed across Utah's wildlands — the canyons, the ridgelines, the rangelands, the places where fires start unannounced. These sensors talk to each other. A handful of gateway nodes pass data to the internet. The rest of the network works independently, without relying on cell towers or satellites. Real people, with real hardware, forming a living early warning system that watches the wild when no one else is watching.

Uinta Watch is a **community project** — not a startup play or a government contract — which means anyone can be part of it. You don't need an engineering background or fire-science credentials. Curiosity, a stake in Utah's future, and a willingness to help protect your neighbors, your forests, and your way of life will get you started.

There are many ways to get involved. The easiest is to **run a sensor node**: pre-assembled units work straight out of the box — plug in a temperature/humidity sensor, configure it with a phone app, mount it in your area with a solar panel, and the node joins the network automatically. If you'd rather build your own, we publish open-source designs with instructions, a parts list, and a community chat to help you through the process, whether or not you've built electronics before. Once a node exists, someone has to **deploy and maintain it** — find a location in your wildland community and be the local anchor of the network. You can also **report smoke or fire** through our app, so what you see feeds directly into the early warning system. If you write code, the entire platform — sensor firmware, mobile app, backend — is open source, and React Native and Node.js developers are welcome. And even without hardware or code, you can **educate and organize**: help your neighbors, your neighborhoods, and your local officials understand what Uinta Watch is and why it matters.

Every person in the network is a node. Every person who reports something real makes the system smarter. The strength of Uinta Watch isn't in any single device or any single person. It's in **all of us together**.

## The cost behind the numbers

The data is stark, and it's human at every level: behind every acre burned is a water supply contaminated by ash, behind every destroyed structure is a displaced family, and behind the firefighter killed on the Utah-Colorado border is a community that swore they'd never forget them.

Utah's wildfire season is no longer isolated to summer. In 2026, conditions were dry and dangerous by March. The 2025–2026 winter was the warmest on record with some of the lowest snowpack ever measured. Fire potential was elevated statewide by June — no part of Utah was immune. Climate-driven changes are extending the fire season at both ends, building fuels that have never been drier, and creating conditions where a single lightning strike or a careless ember becomes a disaster.

Meanwhile, 75% of all wildfires in Utah are **human-caused**. Campfires, equipment sparks, vehicle ignitions — they account for the overwhelming majority of fires that threaten communities. Early detection becomes even more critical when most fires are preventable: if someone can report the smoke before it becomes a blaze, resources can reach the ignition point in minutes instead of hours.

## A different kind of watch

Traditional fire detection systems are built top-down — government agencies purchase expensive equipment, deploy it along predetermined corridors, and wait for a signal. That's important and necessary. But it's not sufficient for a state as vast and varied as Utah. There are gaps. There are ridges no tower reaches. There are communities in the wildland-urban interface where the first responder on scene has always been, and will always be, a neighbor.

Uinta Watch fills those gaps with community technology — open, redundant, and human. The same mesh network architecture that lets sensors communicate also lets communities communicate. When a sensor node detects abnormal heat, it's not just sending a signal to a distant server. It's notifying the nearest neighbor, the local fire department, and the volunteer network **at the same time**.

And because the system is solar-powered and designed to work with minimal infrastructure, it doesn't go dark when you need it most — with no cell signal, the mesh still carries the data, and with the power out, the sensors keep watching.

## Open source, open access

Uinta Watch is built on a simple principle: a community network should be **owned by the community**.

Every design we publish is open source. Every sensor node you build can be repaired, modified, and improved by anyone. We don't believe in proprietary lock-in for public safety infrastructure. The more people who can build, deploy, and maintain these sensors, the stronger the network becomes. When you contribute an improvement to the firmware or a new sensor housing design, it belongs to everyone — now and in the future.

This means Uinta Watch isn't dependent on any single organization, grant cycle, or vendor. It belongs to Utah. As long as there are people who care about these wildlands, the network can grow.
